    Bot Detection at a Neobank

    The standard nine-step system-design spine, worked end to end against an adversarial problem — including the three places where the standard spine quietly breaks and what replaces it.

    Prompt
    "Design bot detection"
    Format
    60 min, whiteboard
    Domain
    Mobile-first bank
    Assumed scale
    15M users
    Decisions/day
    ~20M
    Base rate
    0.1% – 60%

    00Open, then run the spine

    Memorise the opener. It buys you the right to run your own structure for the next fifty minutes, and it plants every thread the interviewer will later pull.

    "Bot detection at a bank isn't one classifier — it's a policy system that scores different entities on different clocks. I'll go through it in the usual order: scope and objective, the ML framing, metrics, data and labels, features, model, serving, rollout, monitoring. Two warnings up front about where this problem departs from a normal ranking system. First, the label is not observed — it's delayed by weeks and biased by whatever policy was live at the time. Second, the data-generating process is an adversary who adapts to every response I emit, which breaks the usual A/B and retraining assumptions. So I'll add one step at the end that the standard template doesn't have: designing against the adversary."

    01Clarify & scope

    Business objective first

    Say the objective in money, then translate. Objective: minimise total abuse cost — realised fraud loss, plus regulatory exposure from mule accounts, plus the customer friction we spend catching it — subject to not degrading the experience of legitimate users. Translation: a calibrated probability of abuse per entity per decision point, fed into a policy that picks the cheapest sufficient intervention. Not "classify bots".

    The four infrastructure questions, and what each answer changes

    AskAssume if unansweredWhat it changes
    QPS at the decision points? ~230/s avg, 1k/s peak Two orders below raw event volume — this is why real-time ML is affordable at all
    Latency SLA? p99 60 ms, fail-open Kills synchronous graph traversal; forces the cascade; sets model size
    Data volume & retention? 500M events/day, 2y hot Sets the streaming/batch split and collides with GDPR retention (§09)
    Existing infra? Kafka + a lake, no feature store If there's no feature store, that's phase one — everything else inherits its skew

    Two more that are specific to this problem and worth more than the generic four: do we control the client? (a first-party app means hardware attestation, an enormously strong signal; a public web surface deletes that entire branch), and is any traffic currently unpoliced? (determines whether you have unbiased labels at all).

    Non-goals, stated out loud

    • Not authorised-push-payment fraud — a real human doing real actions is a different problem and will eat the hour.
    • Not AML transaction monitoring, though the graph layer shares infrastructure with it — and saying so is a real platform argument.
    • Not WAF/DDoS; volumetric attacks die at the edge before this system sees them.

    02Frame as ML: label, granularity, loss

    This is the step most candidates skip, and it's where the domain's difficulty actually lives. Answer all three parts explicitly.

    What is the label?

    "Bot" is never observed. There is no ground truth for automation; there are only proxies for harm. So define the label per abuse type, as an entity-level outcome within a horizon:

    • ATO: y = 1 if the account was confirmed compromised within 30 days of the login — via chargeback, customer dispute, or upheld analyst review.
    • Farm signup: y = 1 if the account was closed for abuse, or is in a confirmed ring, within 90 days of opening.
    • Negatives are matured non-events: an account with no adverse outcome after the full horizon — not merely "not yet flagged".

    Say the consequence out loud: the horizon means your freshest month of data is only partially labelled, so the training cut-off has to lag the horizon, and you are always training on a slightly stale adversary. That tension — freshness versus label completeness — is a design decision, not an inconvenience.

    What is one training example?

    One row per (entity, decision event, timestamp), with every feature reconstructed as of that timestamp. Not one row per account — an account generates a row at every login, transfer and settings change, and the label is the entity outcome propagated back to each decision within the horizon. Three consequences worth naming:

    • Grouped splitting. Rows from one account — and one ring — must not straddle train and test, or you leak through shared devices. Split by time and by entity cluster.
    • Row weighting. A heavy user generates 50× the rows of a light one and would dominate the loss. Weight down within entity, or sample per entity.
    • Separate models per surface. Onboarding and logged-in traffic have such different feature availability that one model serving both is a compromise for both.

    What is the loss?

    Cost-weighted binary cross-entropy — the default
    Per-example weight = the euro consequence from §03, so a €3,000 ATO outweighs a €5 card-test. This is how the business objective enters the optimisation rather than being bolted on at the threshold.
    Negative downsampling with prior correction
    At 0.1% prevalence, downsample negatives to ~1:20 for tractable training — then correct the intercept back (or fit isotonic on an un-downsampled window), or every probability you emit is inflated and every threshold in your policy layer is wrong. Volunteering the correction, not just the downsampling, is the detail interviewers listen for.
    Why not focal loss by reflex
    It's the standard imbalance answer and it's a poor fit here: it deliberately distorts the probability scale, and this entire system depends on calibrated probabilities feeding an expected-cost policy. Use it only if you're ranking a review queue, where order matters and calibration doesn't.
    A ranking head for the queue
    The analyst queue is a genuine ranking problem with a hard k — optimise it with a LambdaRank-style objective on precision@k, separate from the calibrated classification head. Multi-task: shared trunk, one calibrated head for policy, one ranking head for triage.

    Prediction units — five, not one

    UnitQuestionLatencyWeakness
    RequestIs this call automated?<10 msAlmost no context
    SessionIs a human driving this?<100 msCold start on new devices
    DeviceIs this a farm asset?<100 msEmulator + reset defeats naïve versions
    AccountIs the owner operating it?<300 msUseless at day zero
    Cluster / ringIs this coordinated?min–hoursNot real-time; graph must have formed

    The last row wins interviews. A well-run farm account is individually indistinguishable from a cautious new customer; it is only anomalous relative to its cohort — a hundred accounts opened in forty minutes from four devices, funded by two cards, all paying one beneficiary. No per-account model recovers that.

    The core decision rule

    Choose the action a minimising E[cost] = p(abuse|x)·harm(a) + (1−p(abuse|x))·friction(a) + opcost(a). One calibrated probability, many thresholds — one per action, per surface, per value band. This is why calibration matters more than ranking, and why the single global threshold most candidates propose is the wrong answer.

    03Metrics

    Money first, then offline ML metrics, then online, then guardrails. The generic list — AUC, NDCG@k, CTR, retention — mostly doesn't survive contact with this problem, and saying which parts don't and why is worth more than reciting it.

    The cost model, worked

    • 4.5M logins/day; steady-state ATO base rate ~0.05%, spiking above 50% of login traffic during a stuffing campaign. Quote both — the base rate is not a constant, and that alone breaks fixed-threshold designs.
    • Realised loss per successful ATO: €600 blended.
    • Step-up on a legitimate user: €0.02 direct plus ~0.4% session abandonment → €0.25 blended.
    • So a step-up pays at p ≈ 0.25/600 ≈ 0.04% — fire challenges generously. A freeze at €180 expected cost needs p ≈ 30% — fire those stingily.

    That arithmetic — two thresholds three orders of magnitude apart from one calibrated score — is worth more than any architecture slide.

    Offline

    Recall @ fixed FPR — the headline
    "Recall at 0.1% FPR", reported per abuse cohort. Fix the FPR because that's the customer-harm budget the business actually agreed to.
    PR-AUC, not ROC-AUC
    At 0.1% prevalence a model hits 0.99 ROC-AUC and is still useless: ROC normalises by a negative class 1,000× larger, hiding exactly the errors that hurt. One sentence, and it lands.
    Precision @ k, where k = review capacity
    The template's precision@k, with a hard operational meaning: 40 analysts × 60 cases/day = 2,400. Everything ranked below 2,400 is invisible to the business.
    Calibration — first-class, not a footnote
    Reliability curves and expected calibration error on matured labels. In a ranking system calibration is optional polish; here the policy layer is the product, so drifting calibration silently moves every threshold you own.
    Time-to-detect and money-at-risk-before-detect
    Catching a mule ring on day 30 after €400k has moved is not a success. Report detection latency distributions and cumulative exposure, not just terminal accuracy.
    NDCG@k — deliberately not used
    There's no graded relevance and no user scanning a list top-down. The queue is ranked by expected recovered euro, which is precision@k weighted by value, not NDCG.

    Online

    CTR and retention don't apply; the online metrics are loss and friction.

    • Realised fraud loss per €M transacted, trended weekly.
    • Attacker cost per successful account, from the red team (§10) — the closest thing to a north star.
    • Treated-vs-holdout realised loss — the only clean causal attribution you will ever have.

    Guardrails that can halt a rollout

    • Challenge rate for known-good users (tenure > 12 months, no prior flags) — hard ceiling.
    • p99 decision latency and fallback rate.
    • Appeal volume and appeal overturn rate — the truest false-positive proxy you own.
    • Support contact rate on auth and payment issues.
    • False-positive rate by customer segment (§09).

    04Data: labels, sampling, leakage, delayed feedback

    Spend eight minutes here. This is where the standard template breaks hardest, and where interviewers separate people who have shipped a risk system from people who have read about one.

    Label sources, and what's wrong with each

    SourceVolumeDelayBias
    Confirmed fraud / chargebackLow7–120 dOnly attacks that succeeded and were reported
    Manual review verdictsMedium1–3 dOnly what current policy already flagged
    Honeypots / canary endpointsLowMinutesPrecise but unrepresentative — catches the curious, not the careful
    Challenge outcomesHighSecondsWeak: humans fail challenges, good bots pass them
    Appeals / disputesLow1–14 dThe cleanest negative label you own — treasure it
    Rule hitsHigh0Circular; training on these teaches the model to imitate the rules

    The three pathologies, and the fixes

    1 · Selective labelling — the one that breaks the template

    You only observe outcomes for traffic you allowed. Everything blocked has no ground truth, and the blocked set is precisely where the model is most uncertain. Train naïvely and you get a model that's excellent at confirming yesterday's policy and blind to anything outside it.

    Fix: a permanently unpoliced random holdout — 0.1–0.5% of traffic that bypasses all enforcement, with a hard value cap and an automatic kill switch on realised loss. Someone in the room will push back on deliberately letting fraud through. Defend it: it is the only source of unbiased prevalence, the only honest denominator for recall, and the only way to answer "how much abuse are we not seeing?" Put the expected monthly leakage in the proposal so it's a decision, not a surprise.

    2 · Feedback loops

    Model blocks a pattern → pattern leaves the data → next model forgets it → attacker returns. Keep blocked events in training with their pre-block labels, decay label weight by age rather than dropping rows, and preserve an exploration budget so the model keeps observing the boundary it enforces.

    3 · Leakage — three specific traps

    • Post-decision features. "Account was frozen" correlates perfectly with the label and doesn't exist at scoring time. Enforce point-in-time correctness in the feature store, not by convention in a notebook.
    • The single-observation leak. "Device seen only once" is a great training feature and a useless serving feature — at scoring time every device that will be seen once has so far been seen once. Any feature involving future counts is poison.
    • Investigation artefacts. Analysts touch fraud cases; "number of internal notes" will top your feature importance and mean nothing.

    Sampling strategy

    • Keep all positives — they're the scarce resource.
    • Downsample negatives to ~1:20, stratified by surface and time bucket so you don't accidentally drop a whole quiet period; correct the prior afterwards (§02).
    • Cap per-entity and per-ring contribution so one 400-account farm doesn't become 8% of the training set and teach the model that farm's idiosyncrasies.
    • Oversample the recent window and the current campaign; time-decay older labels.
    Techniques to name in one sentence each

    PU learning for "confirmed positive / unknown rest". Label propagation across the entity graph — a device with two confirmed mules makes its other accounts strong weak-positives. Rejection inference, borrowed from credit scoring, to reason about the blocked population. Name them and move on; don't lecture.

    Embeddings

    • Sequence embeddings — a session encoder pretrained self-supervised on all 500M events/day with next-event prediction, then fine-tuned. This is how you get value from overwhelming unlabelled data given how scarce labels are.
    • Graph node embeddings — GraphSAGE over the account/device/instrument/beneficiary graph, refreshed every few minutes and written to the online store as plain feature vectors.
    • Consumed as features by the GBDT, not as separate scores. One calibrated probability is far easier to threshold, explain and defend than five scores someone combines by hand.

    Point-in-time correctness

    The template item that holds exactly, and matters more here than almost anywhere: one feature definition, two execution paths (streaming for serving, batch for training), compiled from the same declaration. Training/serving skew in a fraud system doesn't announce itself — it shows up as a model that backtests beautifully and underperforms in production for a quarter before anyone finds it.

    06Model

    Baselines you must beat, in order

    1. Rules only. Velocity ceilings and hot lists. Honest, and stronger than candidates expect — quote it as the incumbent.
    2. Logistic regression on ~30 hand-built features. Trains in seconds, fully explainable, and if the fancy model beats it by two points then the fancy model isn't the story.
    3. GBDT on a few hundred aggregates — the production workhorse.

    Justify going deeper only with measured incremental recall at fixed FPR over the GBDT. "Under two points and it doesn't ship" is a good line, because it puts a falsifiable bar on your own proposal.

    Two-stage retrieval + ranking, translated

    The template's retrieval/ranking split maps twice here, and saying both mappings shows you understand the pattern rather than the vocabulary:

    • On the hot path it becomes a cost cascade: cheap models score all traffic, expensive models run only on the uncertain band. Same economics as retrieval + ranking — reduce the candidate set before spending compute — driven by latency rather than corpus size.
    • Off the hot path it's literally retrieval + ranking: retrieve suspicious entities via ANN over embeddings and graph expansion from known-bad seeds, then rank the shortlist by expected recovered euro for the 2,400-case analyst queue.
    L0
    Deterministic rules & hot lists

    Known-bad devices, seized proxy ranges, velocity ceilings, hard KYC gates. Unfashionable and non-negotiable: they respond in minutes during an incident when a retrain takes days, they're trivially explainable to a regulator, and they're the fail-open floor when everything else times out. Versioned, auditable, editable by risk ops without a deploy — with two-person approval and a mandatory expiry on every rule, or you'll accumulate a thousand nobody dares delete.

    3 ms
    ~35% of catches
    minutes to update
    L1
    Gradient-boosted trees — the workhorse

    LightGBM over a few hundred features: device fan-out, account velocity, deviation from own baseline, network reputation, attestation, funding reuse, plus the L3/L4 embeddings as columns. Handles missingness natively, trains in minutes, explains with SHAP, and beats deep learning on tabular data at this scale. Ship this first; it gets most of the value.

Output goes to the review queue, never to auto-enforcement. Its job is shortening time-to-first-label on a novel campaign from weeks to days.

    Precompute vs. real-time — three freshness tiers

    TierFreshnessContentsWhy not fresher
    Streaming< 1 sVelocity counters, session state, device fan-outAlready as fresh as it can be
    Near-line1–10 minGraph components, node embeddings, ring featuresGraph algorithms don't fit a 60 ms budget — the most common architectural mistake here
    BatchDailyLong-horizon baselines, peer-group stats, novelty modelsStable by nature; cheap to compute once

    Caching — and one thing not to cache

    • Cache features, not decisions. A cached feature is a freshness tradeoff; a cached decision is an exploitable one — an attacker who finds a request shape that caches "allow" replays it for the TTL.
    • Per-entity feature bundles with short TTLs (seconds) and negative caching for absent entities.
    • Third-party intel lookups (proxy scores, bureau data) cached for hours — they're slow, priced per call, and change slowly.
    • Model artefacts and embedding tables pinned in memory; never fetch them per request.

    ANN index — where it genuinely applies

    HNSW over device and session embeddings, rebuilt near-line. Two uses: a nearest-known-bad feature (cosine distance to the closest confirmed-abusive entity, looked up in ~2 ms and fed to L1), and retrieval for the review queue — given one confirmed farm account, pull its 500 nearest neighbours for an analyst to sweep as a cohort. The second is how a single confirmed case turns into a few hundred labels in an afternoon, which is the cheapest label-generation mechanism in the whole design.

    Scale arithmetic

    • 15M users, ~30% DAU → 4.5M DAU; ~60 client events/active day → ~270M/day, plus server-side → ~500M events/day ≈ 6k/s avg, 25k/s peak.
    • Scored decision points — logins, transfers, signups, sensitive settings — ~20M/day ≈ 230/s avg, ~1k/s peak.
    • At 1k/s and 8 ms CPU per L1 inference: ~8 cores of model serving. The cost is in the streaming aggregates and the online store, not the model.
    • ~1 KB/event → 500 GB/day → ~180 TB/year raw, ~40 TB/year columnar-compressed.
    • Degradation ladder: feature-store timeout at 40 ms → serve on partial features with a degraded-confidence flag; model unavailable → L0 rules only; spine lagging → the policy shifts conservative on value actions but never fails closed on a payment path.

    08Evaluation & rollout

    Offline replay

    • Never random splits. Time-based, and ideally out-of-time and out-of-campaign: hold out an entire attack campaign to estimate generalisation to attacks you haven't seen. Random splits leak the future through shared devices and rings and will overstate performance badly.
    • Point-in-time reconstruction of every feature. If you can't reconstruct it, the offline numbers are fiction.
    • Decision replay. Run the candidate over logged traffic and diff against production: what changes, for whom, at what euro cost. This diff persuades a risk committee more than any metric.
    • Counterfactual evaluation on the holdout via inverse-propensity weighting — unbiased estimates of what a new policy would catch. Second payoff of that expensive holdout.

    The ramp

    1. Shadow — scores everything, enforces nothing, ≥2 weeks. Compare score distributions and decision diffs, not just aggregates.
    2. Canary at 1%, with automatic rollback wired to the §03 guardrails.
    3. Ramp 5 → 25 → 50%, holding a permanent 1–2% control for as long as you can defend it.
    4. Long-run holdout for attribution — without it, in twelve months nobody can say what the system is worth and the budget conversation goes badly.
    Where A/B breaks — say this unprompted

    Standard A/B assumes no interference between arms. Here the attacker is a shared entity across both arms: they probe treatment, learn, and change behaviour in control too. Ring abuse also violates independence at the unit level. Mitigations: randomise at the entity-cluster level, use switchback designs over time windows for enforcement changes, and treat any measured effect as a lower bound that decays as the attacker adapts. Volunteering this is a clear staff signal.

    Red teaming

    An internal attack team with a real budget for emulator farms, residential proxies and anti-detect tooling, run quarterly against production. Their cost-per-successful-account is your best north-star, because it measures the thing you're actually trying to change. Add a bug-bounty category for automation bypass. Assume anything you don't test, the adversary will.

    09Monitoring & iteration

    What you watch, in priority order

    • Action rates by type and segment — fastest indicator of trouble, and the one that correlates with customer pain.
    • Score distribution (PSI vs. a fixed reference), per surface. Distribution shift precedes metric shift by days and labels by weeks.
    • Feature health — null rate, cardinality, staleness, p99 fetch, sliced by app version. The most common real incident isn't drift; it's a mobile release that silently stops emitting a field, degrading the model for 30% of users for three weeks before anyone notices.
    • Training/serving skew — log served feature vectors, replay a daily sample through the offline path, alert on divergence. Catches a class of bug nothing else catches.
    • Calibration drift — reliability curve on matured labels, weekly.

    Position bias → enforcement bias

    The template's position-bias item has an exact analogue, and drawing the parallel explicitly plays well. In ranking, what the user saw depends on where you put it, so you correct with propensity weights. Here, what outcome you observe depends on what action you took — a challenged session that fails is not evidence of a bot, it's evidence you challenged it. The fix is the same machinery: log the action propensity at decision time, weight by inverse propensity in training and evaluation, and keep the random holdout as the unweighted reference.

    Two clocks of drift, two different responses

    DriftTimescaleSignatureResponse
    Campaign onsetMinutes–hoursSharp spike in one segment; bimodal scoresL0 rules + hot list. Not a retrain
    Evasion adaptationDays–weeksRecall decays while volume holdsFeature work + targeted retrain on recent labels
    Population driftMonthsFeature distributions move; FPR creeps upScheduled retrain, recalibration, threshold review
    Product driftPer releaseNew flows, new events, coverage cliffsData contracts, release-gated feature validation

    Retraining is not the answer to fast drift. Candidates reflexively say "retrain frequently". A daily retrain does nothing about an attack that starts at 02:00 and takes 40,000 accounts by 06:00 — that's what the rules lane and automated hot-list promotion exist for.

    Retraining triggers

    • Scheduled: L1 daily, L2 weekly, subject to label maturity.
    • PSI breach on the score distribution, or on any top-20 feature.
    • Recall decay on matured labels versus the frozen reference set.
    • A newly curated campaign label set landing.
    • Every trigger passes a human gate when the candidate shifts the score distribution beyond a set threshold — see poisoning, §10.

    Incident playbook — "03:00, credential stuffing spike"

    1. Detect. Login failure and challenge rates breach; page.
    2. Contain (minutes). Raise friction globally on the affected surface — a blunt instrument, deliberately. Better to challenge everyone for twenty minutes than lose accounts.
    3. Characterise (30 min). Slice by ASN, device model, app version, target cohort. Usually one or two dimensions separate it cleanly.
    4. Targeted rules (1 h). Replace blunt friction with narrow L0 rules, each with an expiry attached at creation.
    5. Label (24 h). Curate the campaign into a named evaluation set.
    6. Model (1 week). Retrain including the campaign, verify it catches the attack without the rules, then retire the rules.
    7. Review. Missing signal, missing feature, or threshold problem? Three entirely different fixes — conflating them is how teams accumulate rules forever.

    10The step the template is missing: the adversary

    Every other ML system has a stationary-ish data-generating process. This one has an intelligent agent optimising against you, and almost nothing in the standard spine accounts for it.

    Every response you emit is a training signal for them

    If a blocked attempt fails instantly and distinctively, you've built the attacker a free oracle: they binary-search your boundary at zero cost, overnight. Countermeasures, all cheap:

    • Ambiguous failures — a detected bot sees the same generic error a network timeout produces.
    • Delayed, batched enforcement — let detected accounts run degraded, then remove a whole cohort at once, so the attacker can't attribute failure to a specific behaviour change. This destroys their feedback loop, which is the highest-leverage cheap defence you have.
    • Randomised thresholds in a narrow band, so probing returns noisy answers.
    • Honeypot surfaces no legitimate client touches.
    • Never expose model reasons externally. Reason codes are for analysts and regulators, never for the blocked party.

    Poisoning — why automated retraining is dangerous

    If labels flow automatically into training, an attacker who understands the loop can shape it: appeal aggressively to inject false negatives, or run benign-looking traffic to shift the boundary before the real attack. Defences: quarantine auto-labels behind a review sample; cap any single entity cluster's influence on a training set; require a human gate on retrains that move the score distribution materially; and always backtest against a curated frozen attack set no automated process can touch.

    The economic frame — close the section on this

    "The goal isn't catching every bot. It's making the marginal cost of a successful attack exceed its marginal value. That reframes the roadmap: I'd rather ship a change that adds €4 of cost per fraudulent account than one that adds three points of recall against an attacker who adapts in a fortnight. It's also the number I'd take to a board — attacker unit economics, not model AUC."

    11Legal, fairness, and the human cost

    At a European bank this is a design constraint, not a footnote, and raising it unprompted signals you've shipped something that touched real customers.

    GDPR Art. 22 — automated decisions with significant effect
    Freezing a bank account qualifies: you need a lawful basis, meaningful information about the logic, and a route to human review. Practical consequence: challenges and step-ups can be fully automated; account closure needs a human in the loop — which is why case management is a first-class component and review capacity is a modelling constraint.
    Special-category data
    Behavioural biometrics and face-match embeddings can constitute biometric data under Art. 9. Scope it with legal before it's in the model; prefer derived scores over stored raw biometric signals.
    Purpose limitation & retention
    Fraud-prevention data can't be silently reused for marketing models, and retention limits collide directly with wanting years of training data. Resolve it explicitly with aggregated or pseudonymised long-horizon features rather than quietly keeping raw events forever.
    AML obligations and tipping-off
    Where suspicion is reported to an FIU you may be legally prohibited from telling the customer why they were restricted. Reason codes and customer messaging must handle a state where the true reason cannot be disclosed.

    Disparate impact — measure it or you're shipping it

    • Cheap or older Android devices fail attestation more often → a device-price proxy for income.
    • VPN users and customers behind heavy CGNAT look like proxy traffic.
    • Expats and frequent travellers trip geo-velocity rules constantly.
    • Accessibility tooling looks automated. Screen readers, switch access and voice control produce input patterns behavioural models learn to call "non-human". This is the one people miss and the one where the harm is most acute.

    So: track FPR by device tier, region, account age and accessibility-service usage; alert on the ratio between segments, not the aggregate; exclude or counter-weight pure proxies; and keep the escalation policy asymmetric — cheap actions can be liberal, expensive ones must clear a higher bar for exactly these populations.

    Explainability

    SHAP in the analyst console; a stable human-readable reason-code taxonomy; model cards documenting training data, known weaknesses and evaluated segments; and an appeal path that both restores the customer and writes a high-quality negative label back to the label store. The appeal loop is the rare control that improves compliance and model quality through the same mechanism — say that.

    "How would you staff this?"

    A staff-level question disguised as small talk. Three functions, not three silos:

    • Risk platform — features, spine, decision service, serving. Shared with AML and credit; measured on time-to-ship a new signal.
    • Detection — models, labels, evaluation. Measured on cost-weighted catch rate at a fixed friction budget, never on model metrics alone.
    • Risk operations — analysts, rules, thresholds, appeals. Critically, thresholds are owned by risk ops, not ML: the threshold is a risk-appetite decision, the model just supplies a calibrated probability. Getting that boundary right is what stops ML from being blamed for policy and policy from tuning models by feel.

    13Follow-up probes, with answers

    "You have no labels at all. Day one. What do you do?"
    Hard constraints, then clustering, then a seeded queue. Start with rules needing no labels (impossible velocity, failed attestation, one device with 50 accounts) — near-certain positives that seed the set. Cluster on device/behaviour embeddings and route the densest, most self-similar clusters to analysts; farms produce populations too alike to be organic. Every verdict is a label. Deploy honeypots for precise positives. A usable supervised model in six to eight weeks — and the sequencing is the answer, not the algorithm.
    "Recall dropped 20% overnight. Walk me through it."
    Cheapest-first, because the answers differ completely. (1) Is it real? Label maturity dropping recall is an artefact, not an incident. (2) Feature health — a null-rate cliff tied to an app release is the single most likely cause. (3) Score distribution — did it move (data problem) or hold while outcomes changed (attacker adapted)? (4) Serving path — model version, skew, timeouts. (5) Only then conclude evasion and look at what the misses share. Naming the ordering matters more than naming the cause.
    "How do you handle a brand-new user with no history?"
    Cold start is the norm in signup abuse, not an edge case. Use what exists at t₀: attestation, device reputation from the graph (new to this account, not to the platform), funding provenance, network intel, onboarding sequence dynamics. Separate model for the onboarding surface — feature availability differs so much that one model for both compromises both.
    "The business says false positives are too high. What do you do?"
    First establish what they mean — measured overturn rate, or three loud complaints? If real: don't touch the model first. Move the threshold on the specific expensive action — same-day, quantifiable tradeoff. Then check whether FPs concentrate in a segment (§11); they usually do, and a targeted fix beats a global threshold move. Only then the model. And make them quote the tradeoff back in the same units: this move costs N extra ATOs per week.
    "Why not just buy a vendor?"
    Do, for parts. Device fingerprinting and attestation are commodity, high-fixed-cost and network-effected — buy. The entity graph, feature platform and policy layer are where your data is unique and your differentiation lives — build. The failure mode of buying end-to-end is that your risk appetite gets set by someone else's model and you can't explain a decision to a regulator without a vendor on the call.
    "How do you know the system is working?"
    Three numbers, and only the third is causal. (1) Realised fraud loss per €M transacted, trended. (2) Attacker cost per successful account, from the red team. (3) Holdout-vs-treated realised loss — the only clean attribution you'll ever have, and the second reason to fight for the holdout.
    "What breaks first at 10× traffic?"
    Not the model — inference is embarrassingly parallel. The streaming aggregates and online store go first, specifically hot keys: a shared ASN or a popular device model becomes a partition hotspot. Fixes: salting, tiered aggregation, and sketches (Count-Min, HyperLogLog) for high-cardinality counters. The graph layer is second, and it's the one that forces an architectural change rather than a scaling one.
    "Would an LLM help here?"
    Not on the hot path — latency and cost per decision rule it out, and tabular models win on this data. Where it earns its place: analyst assistance (summarising a case from a hundred events), free-text triage (support tickets, appeal reasons, transaction memos — genuinely under-used for mule detection), and rule drafting from an analyst's description of a campaign, subject to review. Volunteering the boundary reads better than either enthusiasm or dismissal.
    "What if this were a public website, not an app?"
    Lose attestation — the biggest single downgrade — and most device stability. Compensate with TLS/JA4 and HTTP/2 fingerprinting, proof-of-work to add per-request cost, much heavier reliance on graph and funding signals, and a stricter edge rate-limit posture. Accept a materially worse operating point and say so; pretending the same recall is reachable is the wrong answer.
    "What's the biggest risk in your own design?"
    Have a real answer ready. Mine: the feature platform is on the critical path for everything — if online/offline parity isn't right from week one, every later model inherits the skew and the debugging cost compounds. Second: the holdout is politically fragile, it's the first thing cut in a bad quarter, and losing it blinds the evaluation in a way nobody notices for months.
