00The running example

Abstract inference design goes vague fast, so everything below is grounded in one concrete system — an Expedia-style online travel agency adding LLM features to its booking funnel. It exercises every mechanism in this document, and it does so for reasons visible in the product rather than invented for the exercise.

Property of the workloadWhy it drives the design
Enormous prefix reuseEvery conversation carries the same multi-thousand-token system prompt, tool schemas, and policy text; every chat about one property re-reads the same description and reviews. The strongest argument for prefix caching in the whole design.
Long prompts, short outputs“A 4-star hotel in Lisbon under €200 with a pool” injects search results, availability, and pricing — tens of thousands of prompt tokens — and answers in 150. The fleet is prefill-dominated.
Brutal but forecastable traffic~5× diurnal swing, plus January and summer booking peaks. Highly predictable — exactly the condition under which predictive autoscaling beats reactive.
Thin margins, high volumeRevenue per interaction is cents. $/M tokens decides whether the feature ships to 100% of traffic or stays an experiment.
Multilingual, correctness-criticalPrices, dates, and cancellation policies in 30+ languages. A quantization recipe that degrades non-English output or JSON tool calls is a booking-integrity bug, not a benchmark regression.

01Requirements

Workloads

Three classes of traffic want different things from the same GPUs:

ClassExampleDominant SLOShape
Interactivetrip-planning chat, “is this hotel good for toddlers?”TTFT p95, inter-token latencybursty, multi-turn, long shared prefix
Agenticitinerary builder calling search / pricing / availability tools in a loopend-to-end wall clocklong prompts (10k–200k), short outputs, very high prefix reuse
Batchreview summarization for 2M properties, listing enrichment, evalsthroughput and $/tokenlatency-insensitive, schedulable overnight

The agentic tier is the interesting one: “plan me a week in Portugal” may issue 10–20 model calls, each re-sending a growing conversation plus fresh tool output. The user perceives one latency budget covering all of them, so per-call TTFT is roughly 10× more valuable here than the raw number suggests.

SLOs — interactive tier is the binding constraint

  • TTFT p95 ≤ 500 ms at 8k prompt — slower than that and the assistant feels worse than the search box it competes with.
  • ITL / TPOT p95 ≤ 40 ms → ~25 tok/s per stream, comfortably above reading speed.
  • Agentic end-to-end p95 ≤ 8 s for a full multi-tool itinerary turn.
  • Availability 99.9% monthly, degrading by queueing and falling back to classic search results rather than 5xx storms.
  • Goodput is the headline metric: requests/s meeting both TTFT and ITL. Raw tok/s can rise while goodput falls — that trade is the entire scheduling problem.

Non-goals: training, fine-tuning pipelines, model quality work. The platform serves whatever checkpoints it is handed; it owns latency, throughput, and cost.

02The one mechanism everything follows from

Autoregressive decoding has two phases with opposite performance characteristics. Nearly every decision in this document is a consequence of that split — and the two colours below are used consistently throughout the page.

Phase 1 · compute-bound

Prefill

Processes the whole prompt in one forward pass. A big GEMM over n tokens, with attention O(n²) in prompt length.

One 8k-token prefill saturates a GPU by itself. Batching adds little; the tensor cores are already busy.

Phase 2 · memory-bound

Decode

Generates one token per sequence per step, reading every weight in the model to produce a handful of tokens. Arithmetic intensity near 1.

At batch 1 the tensor cores sit ~99% idle. Batching is nearly free and nearly linear.

The floor on a decode step for a dense model:

step_time ≥ (model_bytes + kv_bytes_read) / HBM_bandwidth

For 70B in FP8 (70 GB) sharded TP=8 across H100s (3.35 TB/s each), every GPU reads ~8.75 GB per step → a ~2.6 ms floor: ~380 tok/s single-stream in theory, perhaps 100–150 in practice after kernel launch, all-reduce, and sampling overhead.

The crucial consequence: that weight read is amortized across the whole batch. Going from batch 1 to batch 64 costs almost nothing in step time and yields 64× the tokens. Throughput is roughly linear in batch size until KV reads start to rival weight reads, or you cross into compute-bound territory.

The crossover — the critical batch size — is the hardware's FLOP:byte ratio. An H100 does ~989 dense BF16 TFLOPS against 3.35 TB/s, i.e. ~295 FLOP per byte. So you need roughly 256–512 tokens in flight before decode stops being bandwidth-starved. Below that you are buying GPU time and throwing it away.

Everything below serves one goal: keep several hundred tokens in flight per GPU without blowing the KV cache budget or violating the latency SLO.

04KV cache

4.1 Sizing — the number that governs concurrency

kv_bytes_per_token = 2 (K,V) × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × dtype_bytes
ModelLayersKV headshead_dimFP16 / token8k context
8B, GQA-8328128128 KiB1.0 GiB
70B, GQA-8808128320 KiB2.5 GiB
13B, full MHA4040128800 KiB6.3 GiB

Note the third row. Grouped-query attention is a ~5–10× KV reduction and the single largest lever on concurrency; full-MHA models are dramatically more expensive to serve at long context than their parameter count suggests. Multi-head latent attention compresses further by caching a low-rank latent instead of full K/V.

On 8×H100 (640 GB) serving 70B in FP8: ~70 GB weights, ~40 GB activations and workspace and fragmentation, leaving ~530 GB for KV → ~1.6M tokens → ~200 concurrent requests at 8k context. That number is your per-replica concurrency limit, and it is what the autoscaler ultimately scales.

4.2 Paged allocation

Never allocate a contiguous per-request buffer sized to max_tokens. That was the original sin of first-generation servers: reserving for the worst case wasted 60–80% of the cache to internal fragmentation.

Instead, PagedAttention: KV lives in fixed-size blocks (typically 16 tokens), a per-request block table maps logical positions to physical blocks, and attention kernels gather through that indirection. Fragmentation drops below 4%, and blocks become shareable:

  • Copy-on-write for parallel samples — n=4 completions share the prompt's blocks until they diverge.
  • Prefix caching — a radix tree over token-ID prefixes lets a new request adopt existing blocks by reference. Cache-hit prefill is the difference between a 3 s and a 200 ms TTFT on long-context agent loops.

Why this dominates the OTA design

Structure the prompt so the shared part comes first and the volatile part last. Prefix caching matches on exact token prefixes, so one early-inserted timestamp invalidates everything after it:

System prompt + tool schemas + policy text~3k tok · 100% of traffic
Destination / property context, reviews~20k tok · per destination
Conversation historyvariable · per session
Live availability + pricing + user query~2k tok · unique, must be last

Three tiers of reuse fall out of that layout: the system block is cached fleet-wide, the property block per popular destination (travel demand is extremely Zipfian — a few hundred destinations cover most traffic), and the history block makes turn n+1 nearly free. In an agentic loop, each of the 10–20 calls appends to a prefix the replica already holds, so only the newest tool result needs prefilling. That is an 8-second itinerary turn instead of a 40-second one, and it costs nothing but prompt discipline.

4.3 Cache-aware routing

Prefix caching only pays if the request lands on the replica holding the prefix. Round-robin destroys most of the benefit.

Route on a hash of the request's prefix, with bounded-load consistent hashing: prefer the cache-owning replica, but fall through when it exceeds ~1.25× mean load. The bounded-load part is load-bearing rather than theoretical here — destination popularity is Zipfian and seasonal, so “everyone is asking about Lisbon this week” is routine. Naive prefix hashing would pin that spike to one replica while the fleet idles.

Otherwise prefer least-outstanding-tokens over least-connections: requests are wildly unequal in cost, so counting them is nearly meaningless.

4.4 Eviction, tiering, and pressure

  • Eviction — LRU over reference-counted blocks; never evict blocks of a running sequence.
  • Tiering — spill cold prefix blocks HBM → host DRAM (~50 GB/s over PCIe) → local NVMe. Recomputing a 100k-token prefill costs seconds of GPU; re-reading 30 GB of KV costs ~3 s and no GPU. Worth it for large reused contexts, not for short prompts where recompute is cheaper than transfer.
  • Preemption — when the cache fills, either swap a sequence's KV to host memory or drop and recompute on resume. Recompute wins for short sequences, swap for long ones. Either way, preemption rate is a first-class alert: a steady rate means admission control is over-admitting and the system is burning GPU on repeated work.
  • KV quantization — FP8 KV roughly doubles concurrency for a small quality cost, near-free on Hopper+. INT4 KV is riskier: quantize K per-channel, V per-token, and validate on long-context retrieval where KV error compounds worst.

4.5 Free the cache aggressively

An underrated source of waste: a client disconnects mid-stream and the server keeps generating. Propagate cancellation from the HTTP layer to the scheduler and free the blocks immediately. On a product with a visible “stop” button, this can be several percent of total GPU spend.

05Continuous batching and scheduling

5.1 Iteration-level scheduling

Static batching — collect N requests, run to completion — is pathological when outputs vary from 5 to 4000 tokens: the batch runs at the speed of its longest member and the GPU drains to a trickle as sequences finish.

Continuous (in-flight) batching reschedules at every decode step. Finished sequences leave immediately, queued ones join in their place, and batch composition changes every ~20 ms. Typically a 5–20× throughput improvement at the same latency, and the single highest-leverage thing in the serving stack.

5.2 Chunked prefill — the interference problem

Prefill and decode compete. A newly arrived 32k-token prompt occupies the GPU for hundreds of milliseconds; every decoding sequence stalls for that window and its ITL spikes. Users see the stream stutter whenever someone else asks a long question.

Chunked prefill splits the prompt into fixed chunks (512–2048 tokens) and co-schedules one chunk alongside the decode batch each iteration, under a global per-step token budget:

tokens_this_step = decode_tokens (1 per running seq) + prefill_chunk_tokens ≤ budget

This is also what makes the batch compute-efficient: decode alone rarely reaches the critical batch size, but decode + a prefill chunk does, so the free tensor-core capacity during decode is spent on prefill instead of idled.

The trade-off is explicit and tunable: bigger chunks → better TTFT, worse ITL. Set the budget from your SLO ratio, and consider different budgets for latency-sensitive and throughput-sensitive tiers.

5.3 Admission control and queueing

  • Queue discipline — FCFS is predictable and starvation-free; priority classes (interactive > agentic > batch) with aging bound worst-case wait. Shortest-job-first is tempting, but output length is unknown in advance and estimating it invites starvation of long generations.
  • Admission — reject or queue when projected TTFT already exceeds SLO. Failing fast at the door beats admitting a request that will miss its target and degrade everyone else's.
  • KV reservation — admit only if the expected KV footprint fits with headroom. Optimistic admission plus preemption works, but only with a preemption budget; unbounded optimism produces thrash.
  • Per-tenant token buckets — rate-limit on tokens per second, not requests per second. A request is not a unit of cost.

5.4 Prefill/decode disaggregation

At scale, run prefill and decode on separate pools and ship KV blocks between them over NVLink/RDMA. They have different optimal parallelism (prefill likes TP for latency; decode likes DP replicas with big batches), different SLOs, and different scaling curves. It eliminates interference structurally rather than papering over it with chunk-size tuning.

The OTA workload is exactly that prefill-heavy case — 20k prompt tokens against 150 output tokens is a ~130:1 ratio — and so the strongest realistic argument for disaggregation. But a high prefix-cache hit rate removes most of that prefill work. Measure the post-caching ratio, not the raw one, and exhaust cheap prompt discipline before buying a second pool and a second autoscaler.

5.5 Speculative decoding

A small draft model (or n-gram lookup, or EAGLE/Medusa heads) proposes k tokens; the target model verifies them in one forward pass and accepts the longest correct prefix. Since verification is one pass over the weights, accepting 3 tokens costs about what generating 1 did — a 2–3× single-stream latency win at 70–80% acceptance.

The caveat, and a good interview discriminator: speculation spends spare compute. It helps exactly when you are memory-bound — low batch, latency-critical, off-peak. At high batch you are already compute-bound, and the wasted verification FLOPs on rejected tokens make throughput worse. Gate it dynamically on current batch size rather than enabling it globally.

06Quantization

6.1 Match the format to the bottleneck

The mistake is treating quantization as one dial. Three distinct things can be quantized, and they buy different things:

WhatSavesHelps which phase
WeightsHBM capacity + per-step read volumedecode (bandwidth-bound)
Activationsenables low-precision tensor coresprefill (compute-bound)
KV cacheHBM capacityconcurrency — i.e. everything

Hence W4A16 (INT4 weights, BF16 activations — AWQ/GPTQ) shrinks weights 4× and is excellent for low-batch decode and for fitting a big model on fewer GPUs. But it dequantizes to BF16 inside the kernel, so at high batch — where you are compute-bound — it can be slower than FP8. It is a latency and capacity optimization, not a throughput one.

W8A8 FP8 (E4M3) is the current production default on Hopper: 2× on both weight bytes and tensor-core throughput, near-lossless with light calibration, natively supported so there is no dequant tax. On Blackwell, block-scaled 4-bit (NVFP4/MXFP4) extends the “helps both phases” property down to 4 bits.

Default policy: FP8 weights + FP8 activations + FP8 KV for interactive serving; INT4 weight-only when the goal is fitting a larger model on cheaper GPUs or serving at low concurrency.

6.2 Getting it right

  • Granularity — per-tensor is fastest; per-channel (weights) / per-token (activations) is more accurate; group-wise (g=128) for INT4. Finer granularity costs scale-lookup overhead in the kernel.
  • Outliers — a handful of activation channels have huge dynamic range and dominate the error. AWQ keeps the most salient ~1% of weight channels in higher precision; SmoothQuant migrates activation scale into weights. This is why naive round-to-nearest INT4 falls apart and the published methods don't.
  • Calibration — a few hundred samples from your traffic, not WikiText. A model calibrated on generic English and served on 30 languages of hotel policy text, date arithmetic, and price formatting will quantize badly in exactly the places you care about. Weight the calibration set by real language mix.

6.3 Validating it — where teams get burned

Perplexity is a necessary but wildly insufficient check. Quantization damage concentrates in the tails: long-context retrieval, multi-step reasoning, structured output adherence, and non-English performance all degrade well before perplexity moves.

  1. Task evals relevant to production (not just MMLU) with confidence intervals — a 0.5 point move on 1000 items is noise.
  2. Long-context probes at max supported length, framed as the real failure mode: given 20k tokens of reviews, does it still find the line saying the pool is closed for renovation?
  3. Format and tool-call adherence on real request shapes — a malformed search_flights call is a broken product.
  4. A paired A/B against the BF16 baseline on replayed production traffic, greedy decoding, judge-scored. Catches “subtly worse at the thing our users actually do”, which no public benchmark will.
  5. Cheap non-negotiable domain assertions: prices and dates in the response must match the injected tool output exactly. Numeric drift shows up long before bad prose, and on a booking funnel a hallucinated price is the most expensive possible failure.
  6. Sign-off that a quantized checkpoint is a distinct model version in the registry, shipping through the same canary process as a new checkpoint — because it is one.

07Parallelism and placement

  • Tensor parallelism (TP) — splits every layer; an all-reduce per layer means it wants NVLink. Use it to fit the model and cut single-stream latency. Beyond one NVLink domain (typically 8 GPUs), communication dominates.
  • Pipeline parallelism (PP) — splits layers across stages, tolerates slower interconnect, introduces bubbles and hurts latency. Use it to cross node boundaries when TP alone can't fit the model.
  • Data parallelism (DP) — independent replicas. Best throughput-per-dollar whenever the model fits, and the unit the autoscaler manipulates.
  • Expert parallelism (EP) — for MoE, distributes experts across GPUs, and introduces expert load imbalance as a new failure mode needing a rebalancing story.

Rule of thumb: the smallest TP that fits the model with room for a healthy KV cache, then scale out with DP replicas. Reaching for TP=8 on a model that fits in TP=2 buys latency you may not need, and costs throughput and blast radius.

08Autoscaling

8.1 Scale on the right signal

GPU utilization is a bad autoscaling signal for LLM serving. Decode keeps the GPU nominally busy at low arithmetic efficiency, so utilization sits high and flat while headroom varies enormously. Scaling on it produces both under- and over-provisioning.

#SignalWhy
1Queue wait time (p95 arrival → first step)the leading indicator; rises before latency does
2KV cache utilization %at ~85–90%, preemption and admission rejection are imminent
3Running batch vs max concurrencyhow much replica capacity is committed
4SLO burn ratelagging ground truth — for alerting and as a scaling backstop

8.2 Capacity model

Derive concurrency_per_replica empirically: load-test until p95 TTFT or ITL hits the SLO and record the concurrency at that knee. It's a function of model, quantization, context length, and chunk budget — re-derive it whenever any of those change, and store it alongside the engine config. Then it's Little's Law:

required_replicas = ceil( (λ_peak × avg_service_time) / (concurrency_per_replica × target_util) )

$/M_tokens        = (GPUs × $/GPU-hour) / (aggregate_output_tok/s × 3600) × 10^6

Use target_util ≈ 0.7 to absorb burst and rolling deploys. Measure λ in tokens, not requests — mixing a 200-token and a 100k-token workload under one request-rate number will mis-size the fleet by an order of magnitude.

Worked example — sizing the OTA assistant

Peak of 2,000 conversations/minute, 4 turns each, 20k prompt tokens and 150 output tokens per turn:

turns/s        = 2000 × 4 / 60                   ≈ 133/s
raw prefill    = 133 × 20,000                    ≈ 2.7M prompt tok/s
after caching  = 2.7M × (1 − 0.9 hit rate)       ≈ 270k prompt tok/s
output         = 133 × 150                       ≈  20k output tok/s

The 90% prefix-cache hit rate is the whole ballgame: it turns 2.7M into 270k prompt tokens/s — a 10× reduction in fleet size — and it comes from prompt layout and cache-aware routing rather than from buying anything. No other lever here is worth as much on this workload, which is why §11 puts caching in phase 1 and disaggregation in phase 3.

Against a measured ~50k prefill tok/s and ~3k output tok/s per 8×H100 replica: prefill needs ~6 replicas, decode ~7, so ~8 replicas (64 H100s) at 70% target utilization. At $2.50/GPU-h that's ~$160/h at peak; with the 5× diurnal swing and autoscaling, the daily average lands nearer $60–70/h. Against booking volume, that arithmetic is the difference between a feature that pays for itself and one that doesn't.

8.3 Cold start is the hard part

A new replica is not useful for 2–5 minutes: pull the image, load 70–140 GB of weights, allocate and warm the KV pool, capture CUDA graphs, compile kernels. Traffic spikes arrive in seconds. No amount of HPA tuning closes that gap. What does:

  • Weights on node-local NVMe, pre-warmed by a DaemonSet, not pulled from object storage per pod (10 GB/s vs 1–5 GB/s, and no cross-AZ egress).
  • Weights out of the container image — a 140 GB image is an unpullable image.
  • Warm pool / headroom — spare replicas, or low-priority placeholder pods evicted instantly to make room. You trade ~10–20% idle GPU for spike absorption; on interactive traffic that trade is almost always correct.
  • Predictive scaling on the daily and weekly curve, reactive only for the residual. Travel is the best case for this: the ~5× daily swing is smooth and regular, booking-season peaks are known months ahead, and the genuinely spiky events — a fare drop, a destination going viral, weather stranding travellers — are exactly the residual a warm pool should cover. Don't react your way through a curve you can already see coming.
  • Asymmetric thresholds — scale up fast, scale down slowly (10–15 min stabilization). Flapping is expensive when each start costs minutes of GPU time.
  • Queue-based backpressure as the shock absorber covering scale-up latency: a bounded queue with fast rejection converts an overload into a few explicit 429s instead of a fleet-wide latency collapse.

8.4 Scaling the tiers

  • Prefill and decode scale independently when disaggregated — prefill on queue depth and prompt-token rate, decode on KV utilization and running batch.
  • Batch tier on spot with checkpointing and drain-on-preemption; interactive on on-demand. Often the largest single cost lever available, ahead of any kernel work — and it composes with the diurnal curve: review summarization is perfectly happy to run at 04:00 on capacity the interactive tier just gave back.
  • Follow the sun — booking traffic peaks in local evening, so regional peaks are staggered by hours. Let each region scale on its own curve, or route overflow across regions where data residency permits; the second is cheaper but adds latency, so keep it a spillover path, not the default.
  • Scale-to-zero only for cold, non-interactive models. Never for interactive traffic.
  • LoRA multiplexing over a replica per fine-tune — the difference between N GPUs and N×adapters GPUs.

09Observability

Instrument per-phase, not per-request-total, or you can't tell which mechanism regressed:

  • Latency split — queue wait, prefill, per-token decode, detokenize, network as separate histograms. A TTFT regression is one of these, and the split tells you which.
  • Goodput — requests/s meeting both SLOs, sliced by tenant and prompt-length bucket.
  • KV — utilization, prefix-cache hit rate, preemptions/s, swap bytes/s.
  • Batch — running batch size histogram and per-step token budget occupancy. If the histogram sits below the critical batch size, you are paying for idle tensor cores.
  • Cost — $/M tokens, tokens/GPU-hour, idle GPU fraction.
  • Quality — sampled output logging with an offline judge, so a bad quantization or checkpoint rollout is caught by data rather than by users.

Load test with realistic prompt and output length distributions replayed from production. Benchmarks with fixed 128-in/128-out tell you nothing about a system whose behavior is dominated by length variance.

11Rollout plan

Phase 1 — get the fundamentals right (weeks)

Single pool per model on a mature engine. Continuous batching + PagedAttention + prefix caching + chunked prefill. FP8 weights, activations, and KV. Least-outstanding-tokens routing. Autoscaling on queue depth and KV utilization with a warm pool. This configuration captures the large majority of the available performance.

Also in phase 1, and cheaper than any of it: fix the prompt layout — stable prefix first, volatile suffix last — and put prefix-cache hit rate on the release gate. On a prefix-heavy workload that single change outweighs everything else on this list.

Phase 2 — routing and tiering

Prefix-cache-aware routing with bounded load. Priority classes and per-tenant token buckets. Batch tier on spot, scheduled into the overnight trough. Speculative decoding gated on batch size. LoRA multiplexing for fine-tunes.

Phase 3 — specialize, if the numbers justify it

Prefill/decode disaggregation. Tiered KV offload to DRAM/NVMe for long-context reuse. Predictive autoscaling. Per-workload engine tuning.

The ordering is deliberate: every phase-3 item adds a moving part and an on-call burden, and none of them beat getting phase 1 right. The most common expensive mistake in this space is building the disaggregated, tiered, speculatively-decoded architecture before measuring that the simple one was insufficient.

12Quick reference

Formulas

kv_bytes_per_token   = 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × dtype_bytes
decode_step_floor    = (model_bytes + kv_read_bytes) / HBM_bandwidth
critical_batch       ≈ peak_FLOPS / HBM_bandwidth          (H100 ≈ 295)

Talking points

  • Prefill is compute-bound, decode is memory-bound — every design choice follows from this.
  • Batching is free for decode until the critical batch size (~256–512 tokens in flight).
  • PagedAttention cut KV fragmentation from 60–80% to under 4% and made prefix sharing possible.
  • Continuous batching is a 5–20× win over static batching; the highest-leverage change in the stack.
  • GQA/MLA is a 5–10× KV reduction — the biggest lever on concurrency.
  • W4A16 helps decode, not prefill; FP8 helps both. Match the format to the bottleneck.
  • Speculative decoding helps at low batch and can hurt at high batch.
  • Never autoscale on GPU utilization; use queue wait and KV utilization.
  • Cold start is minutes — warm pools and node-local weights, not clever HPA tuning.
  • Optimize for goodput and $/M tokens, not raw throughput.
  • On a prefix-heavy workload, cache hit rate is worth more than every kernel optimization combined: a 90% hit rate is a 10× fleet-size reduction, bought with prompt layout.
  • Put the stable part of the prompt first and the volatile part last — a timestamp in the header invalidates the entire cache behind it.

All figures are illustrative order-of-magnitude estimates for an H100-class fleet, chosen to show the shape of the arithmetic. Measure your own hardware, model, and traffic before committing to any of them.
