The Two-Stage Recommender

A Netflix / YouTube / Spotify class problem, worked end to end: retrieval and ranking, cold start, and the feedback loop the system creates in its own training data. Structured as the nine moves of the interview, in the order you should speak them.

Open with this The catalog is too big to score, so I'll build a funnel: cheap recall first, expensive precision second, and a policy layer last. The hard parts aren't the models — they're the label definition, the feedback loop the system creates in its own training data, and the latency budget that decides how much model I can afford.

STEP 01

Clarify & scope: business objective → ML metric

Business objective. For a subscription product the objective is retention — the probability the member renews next month. For an ad-supported product it is long-term satisfied engagement, where the "long-term" qualifier does all the real work.

The translation problem. Retention is not a trainable label. It is delayed by weeks, near-constant per user (most users renew), confounded by everything outside the product, and impossible to credit-assign to a single impression. So I don't train on it — I decompose:

value(u, i, ctx) = Σ_k  w_k · P(event_k | u, i, ctx)

where the events are things observable per impression — click, valid watch, completion, like, subscribe, share — minus penalty terms for observed dissatisfaction: skip-in-5s, hide, "not interested", report.

The w_k weights are the value model. They are not learned from impression data (nothing in that data says what a "share" is worth); they're set by product judgment and then tuned by A/B against the north-star metric.

Say out loudRetention is the arbiter of the weights, not the training target. Getting that backwards means optimizing the metric you can measure and shipping regressions in the one you care about.

STEP 02

Frame as ML: label, granularity, loss

Two models, two different framings. This is the core of the answer.

Retrieval (candidate generation)

  • Granularity: one positive interaction — (user state at time t, item engaged with). Not one impression.
  • Task: given user state, produce a distribution over the entire catalog — P(next positive item | user context).
  • Loss: sampled softmax over the catalog with a logQ correction. In-batch negatives are sampled proportional to popularity, which systematically penalizes popular items; correcting the logit by the log sampling probability fixes it.
s'(u, i) = s(u, i) − log Q(i | u)
L        = −log softmax over batch of s'(u, ·)

Without it, retrieval quietly over-suppresses head content and the offline/online gap becomes unexplainable. Two lines of code, and a reliable staff-level tell.

The constraint that defines the architecture: the item side must be computable without the user, because item embeddings are precomputed and indexed. That forbids user×item cross features — and that limitation is precisely why a second stage exists.

Ranking

  • Granularity: one impression — (request_id, user, item, position, surface, timestamp, model_version), logged at serve time with the features actually used.
  • Labels: a vector, not a scalar — {clicked, valid_watch, completed, watch_seconds, liked, subscribed, hidden, reported}.
  • Loss: multi-task. Binary heads with weighted cross-entropy; watch time as bucketed cross-entropy over duration bins rather than regression on a heavy tail — it gives a distribution to integrate instead of a conditional mean dominated by outliers.
  • Negatives: impressed-not-engaged. These are hard negatives and exactly the right ones — the ranker's job is separating items retrieval already thinks are plausible.

Say out loudRetrieval and ranking are trained on different data distributions on purpose. Ranking sees only what was shown. Retrieval must see the whole catalog via random negatives, or it never learns that 99.99% of the catalog is irrelevant — and it inherits the previous ranker's blind spots forever.

Calibration deserves emphasis: the moment several heads are combined by weights, relative ordering isn't enough — they must sit on a common probability scale or the weights mean nothing.

Online

  • Primary: a retention proxy that moves inside an experiment window — weekly active days, or DAU/MAU stickiness. Not raw watch time.
  • Secondary: valid watches per DAU, session count, session depth, CTR.
  • Guardrails: hide / "not interested" / report / unsubscribe rates; sessions vs. dwell-per-session moving in opposite directions — the signature of a rabbit-hole regression, where people watch longer per visit but return less; impression Gini across items and creators; share of DAU seeing a new-to-them creator; share of impressions to items under 7 days old; p99 latency, error rate, fallback-serving rate.

Name the trap firstCTR alone produces clickbait; watch time alone produces long, autoplay-y, low-quality content and rabbit holes. The industry answer is a satisfaction signal — an in-product survey on a small random sample, used to train a satisfaction head that is then distilled to full traffic, plus explicit negative feedback as a penalty term.

STEP 04

Data

Sources. Impression/serving logs, playback events, explicit feedback, catalog metadata, content-derived embeddings (text, audio, video frames), user profile, follow graph, search queries.

Non-negotiableLog, at serve time: the full candidate list, positions, per-stage scores, model versions, the feature values actually used, and the propensity for exploration-served slots. None of it is reconstructable later. A recsys with bad serving logs cannot be improved, only replaced.

Labeling — implicit feedback

Label definitions are product decisions with large consequences:

  • Video: valid watch = ≥30s or ≥50% of duration, whichever is shorter — protects short-form from a fixed threshold.
  • Music: a stream at ≥30s, and skip within 30s as an explicit negative — the highest-value label in music recsys.
  • Long-form subscription: title start + ≥70% completion; "started and abandoned within 5 minutes" is a strong negative, not a neutral.

Sampling

Keep all positives, downsample negatives (e.g. 1 in 10) to control cost — then recalibrate. With negative keep-rate r, true odds = sampled odds × r:

p_true = p_s · r / (1 − p_s + p_s · r)

Skipping this is the most common source of "great offline, and the downstream value/ads system behaves insanely in production."

Leakage

  • Split by time, never randomly — train on t < T, evaluate on t ∈ [T, T+1d]. Random splits let the model see the future of the same session and inflate offline metrics by amounts that never materialize.
  • All aggregates must be point-in-time: "user's watch count in this genre" as of the impression, not over the full window.
  • Watch for label-adjacent features — a "recently played" counter updated before the log row is written.

Imbalance

~4% CTR; rare positives (subscribe, share) under 0.1%. Per-head loss weights, and PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC for the rare heads.

Delayed feedback

Click is instant, valid watch arrives in minutes, subscribe and return-visit in hours or days. For hourly incremental training that creates fake negatives — rows labeled 0 that will become 1. Options: an attribution window (wait 4h, accept staleness); a delayed-feedback model that jointly models P(convert eventually) and P(delay ≤ t) and importance-weights the not-yet-converted rows; or fast heads trained online with slow heads on the daily batch. Most candidates never raise this.

Presentation bias

Position, slot size, thumbnail treatment, and the fact the item was shown at all confound every label. Two mitigations, used together:

  • Shallow position tower — position and device feed a small separate tower whose output is added to the logit; at serving, position is pinned to a constant. The main network is forced to learn position-independent relevance.
  • IPS weighting on logged propensities for the exploration-served fraction.

Privacy & compliance

Retention windows on raw events; deletion must propagate into training sets and learned user embeddings — which is a practical argument for deriving user vectors at request time from recent events rather than storing long-lived learned parameters; separate handling for minors; a non-personalized feed option.

Representation. Learned ID embeddings for users, items and creators, with hashing for the tail (accept collisions, monitor the rate); multimodal content encoders frozen or fine-tuned; continuous features quantile-normalized and expanded as x, x², √x — cheap, and consistently helps in these architectures.

Point-in-time, practicallyDon't reconstruct training features from the warehouse — log the served feature vector and train on that, which makes training/serving skew structurally impossible for logged features. For the rest, one shared transformation library compiled into both paths, plus a skew detector that recomputes offline features for 0.1% of logged requests and alerts on divergence. Skew is the number-one cause of "great offline, flat online".

If none apply — a 20k-title licensed catalog, mostly stable — GBDT plus a good candidate set is the right architecture, and going further is résumé-driven engineering.

Why two stages — do the arithmetic

Scoring 80M items at 60k QPS with a ranker costing 1ms per 1,000 items is 4.8×10¹² item-scorings per second. That's not a budget question, it's a physics question.

Candidate funnelitems · latency
eligible catalog 80,000,000
↓retrieval — 8 parallel sources, ANN + rules~20 ms
candidate union 4,000
↓pre-rank — tiny MLP on cached embeddings~15 ms
ranking set 600
↓rank — multi-task DNN, full cross features~70 ms
scored set 600
↓policy — diversity, licensing, dedupe, caps~20 ms
returned feed 30

Each stage is roughly 100× cheaper per item and 10–100× less accurate than the next. The funnel buys accuracy exactly where the candidate set is small enough to afford it.

Retrieval is an ensemble, not one model

A single ANN index is a common wrong answer. Production retrieval is a union of sources with per-source quotas:

  1. Two-tower ANN — user tower computed online from live session state, item tower precomputed and indexed.
  2. Item–item co-visitation seeded from the last few interactions — catches short-term intent the tower smooths away.
  3. Follows / subscriptions — deterministic, high precision.
  4. Continue watching — rule-based, highest precision of all.
  5. Fresh & trending per locale — the only source that can surface items with no interaction history.
  6. Search / intent-derived from recent queries.
  7. Exploration — sampled with logged propensities.

Quotas stop one source starving the others, and per-source recall is monitored independently — when quality drops you need to know which source degraded.

Two-tower specifics worth naming: a shared item encoder over content + ID; L2-normalized embeddings with a learned temperature; sampled softmax with logQ; item embeddings refreshed nightly with incremental updates every few minutes for new items; and the user tower runs at request time, not precomputed — a user's last three actions are the most predictive features available, and a nightly user vector throws them away.

Ranking

Multi-task DNN: a shared bottom (embeddings plus a sequence encoder over the last N items) feeding MMoE-style experts with per-task gates, one head per label, plus the shallow position tower added to the logits and disabled at serving. Per-head isotonic or Platt calibration fitted on held-out recent data and refreshed daily. Final score = value model over calibrated probabilities.

The policy layer people forget

Runs on the top ~600 and is mostly not ML: hard eligibility (licensing, region, age rating, already-watched, policy), near-duplicate collapse, creator and topic caps per page, diversity via MMR or a DPP over item embeddings, freshness boosts, exploration-slot injection, business overrides. Being explicit that hard constraints live here — not in the loss — is a maturity signal: you cannot guarantee a licensing constraint with a soft penalty term.

Cold start

New item — the harder one

  • Day 0, the content tower gives a usable embedding from title, description, audio and frames — the item is retrievable before its first interaction. This is the main reason to pay for a DNN retrieval model at all.
  • Reserve an exploration budget (~3–5% of slots), allocated by Thompson sampling over each item's engagement posterior, with the Beta prior seeded from content-nearest neighbours rather than uniform — that alone cuts exploration cost by an order of magnitude.
  • Graduate at ~1k impressions to a learned ID embedding initialized from the content vector, so there's no quality dip at handover.
  • Incremental ANN inserts every few minutes. A nightly rebuild means news, live and new-release music are structurally broken.

New user

  • Onboarding preference selection where the product allows it; otherwise a prior from locale, language, device and referrer.
  • Because the user tower is computed online, the model conditions on interaction #1 within seconds — the strongest cold-start mechanism, and it falls out of the serving design for free.
  • Shrink personalized estimates toward segment priors, with weight rising in interaction count.
  • Deliberate early diversity — an exploitative feed for a brand-new user learns nothing about them.

New market

Transfer the content encoder, retrain ID embeddings, lean on locale popularity priors, and expect the value-model weights to need re-tuning per market.

Feedback loops

The system trains on data it generated. Untreated, that produces rich-get-richer popularity collapse, a filter bubble per user, and offline metrics that improve while the product gets worse — because the eval set is drawn from the model's own choices.

  1. A random-traffic holdout — 0.5–1% of requests served by a fixed, non-personalized or uniformly-sampled policy. It is the only unbiased evaluation set you will ever have, and the only training data containing items your ranker refuses to show. If the org has one, protect it; if not, buying it is the highest-leverage item on the roadmap.
  2. Logged propensities and off-policy evaluation — IPS, self-normalized IPS, doubly-robust estimators with clipping, so an offline replay of a new policy means something.
  3. logQ and popularity-debiased sampling in retrieval training.
  4. Diversity constraints in the policy layer, which bound bubble formation regardless of what the model wants.
  5. Monitor the loop directly — impression Gini, catalog coverage, share of impressions to items under 7 days old, share of users seeing new creators. These trend badly for months before engagement metrics notice.
STEP 07

Serving

State the latency as a budget, not a hope. p99 = 300 ms:

The two expensive lines are feature hydration and the ranker — which is exactly why a pre-ranker exists, and the first place to attack if the budget tightens.

Precompute vs. real-time

  • Precompute: item embeddings, item features, ANN index, co-visitation tables, segment popularity lists.
  • Real-time: user tower, cross features, ranking, policy.
  • Fully precomputed feeds are right for push notifications and email — no latency constraint, huge batch efficiency — and as a warm fallback. A hybrid (nightly precomputed feed, patched with real-time results for active sessions) is a legitimate cost lever at very high QPS.

Caching

Session-scoped candidate cache with a ~1 min TTL invalidated on new interaction — cheap, because consecutive requests in a session share most of the funnel. Plus an item feature cache (near-100% hit), an embedding cache, and negative caching for ineligible items.

ANN index

80M × 128-dim fp32 = 41 GB, too much per replica — product-quantize (m=32 subquantizers → 32 B/vector ≈ 2.6 GB plus graph) and shard. HNSW where recall and latency dominate, IVF-PQ where memory does. Rebuild nightly, insert continuously. Measure ANN recall against exact search on a sample — an index silently degrades as it's mutated, and nothing else in the funnel will tell you.

Degrade, never emptyranker times out → serve pre-rank order · retrieval fails → cached session candidates · cache miss → precomputed nightly feed · total failure → popularity by locale. Track the fallback rate as a first-class metric — a silently rising one looks exactly like a model regression.

Cost check. 60k QPS × 600 ranked items = 36M scorings per second. At a few hundred FLOPs per item per layer, that sets the ranker's parameter budget directly: model capacity is a function of the latency and cost budget, not of what's fashionable.

STEP 08

Evaluation & rollout

The full ladder, cheapest and least trustworthy first:

  1. Offline replay on logged data with propensity correction (IPS / SNIPS / DR, clipped). Good for eliminating bad ideas; never trust the magnitude.
  2. Interleaving (team-draft) — mix two rankers' results into one list and attribute engagement per source. 10–100× more sample-efficient than an A/B and it controls user-level variance completely. The right intermediate step for ranker comparisons; it cannot measure whole-experience effects like session count.
  3. Shadow deployment — score live traffic, serve nothing. Validates latency, feature coverage, score distribution and calibration under real load.
  4. A/B test. Randomization unit is the user, not the request — request-level randomization contaminates through session state and user learning. Power: with per-user daily watch minutes at cv ≈ 2, detecting a 0.5% relative lift at 80% power needs roughly 16·cv²/δ² ≈ 2.5M users per arm. Worth computing out loud, because it explains why small teams cannot detect the effects they claim. Run ≥2 weeks for novelty decay and weekly seasonality; use sequential or always-valid tests if you need to peek.
  5. Ramp 1% → 5% → 20% → 50% → 100%, with automated rollback on guardrail breach.
  6. Long-term holdback — keep 0.5–1% on the previous system for months. Recsys changes compound, and a year of individually-positive two-week experiments can sum to a negative annual effect.

Two-sided caveat. For changes affecting supply — creator exposure, new-item boosts — user-level randomization leaks through the shared item pool: treatment users' engagement changes the item's popularity features, which affects control users. Use cluster, market or creator-level randomization, and accept the much larger variance.

STEP 09

Monitoring & iteration

Data health. Feature freshness and null rates per feature, schema validation, distribution drift (PSI / KL against the training window), the training-serving skew detector from step 05, and logging completeness — a silently dropped propensity field kills off-policy evaluation a month before anyone notices.

Model health. Daily calibration by slice, score-distribution drift, per-slice user-AUC (new users, new items, market, device), ANN recall vs. exact, and a staleness curve — plot the online metric against days-since-training to derive the retraining cadence empirically instead of guessing it.

Ecosystem health. Impression Gini, catalog coverage, fresh-item impression share, creator concentration, and the exploration budget actually spent — it silently drifts to zero whenever someone "optimizes" the policy layer.

Operational. Model version is a config flip with the last N versions kept warm — rollback in seconds, not a redeploy. Every serving log row carries the model version, or you cannot attribute a regression to anything.

PROBES

Likely follow-ups, with short answers

A user watches one video about X and the feed floods with X.

Three fixes at three layers: topic caps in the policy layer (bounded regardless of scores); time-decay on short-term affinity features so one session can't dominate; and an explicit intent-vs-taste split where short-term intent gets a slot quota rather than the whole page. Measure it with intra-session topic entropy, not engagement.

How would you add ads or sponsored content?

Separate the auction from relevance: the ranker produces a calibrated P(engagement), the ads system produces eCPM = bid × P(click), and a blending policy trades user value against revenue at a tunable exchange rate. This makes calibration a hard requirement — an uncalibrated organic score corrupts the auction.

Offline metrics improved, online is flat. What do you check?

In order: training/serving skew (recompute serving features offline and diff), calibration drift, whether the offline eval set was drawn from the current policy's own impressions, negative-sampling recalibration, position-bias handling, and whether the winning offline slice is a segment with little traffic.

How do you know retrieval, not ranking, is the bottleneck?

Ceiling analysis: replace the ranker with an oracle that reorders the candidate set by observed labels. If oracle ranking of the current candidates barely moves the metric, the good items aren't in the candidate set and retrieval is the bottleneck. Corollary: track the retrieval union's Recall@k against items the ranker puts top-10 in an unconstrained offline run.

Why not one big model?

Because the item tower must be precomputable to build an index. The moment you add user×item cross features you have to evaluate per (user, item) pair, and 80M forward passes per request isn't available. One model is right only when the catalog is small enough to score exhaustively.

Ranking looks great, but engagement dropped for new users.

Expected — the model is trained on data dominated by heavy users, so the loss barely notices new ones. Fix with per-slice evaluation as a ship gate, loss reweighting or a segment-specific model, more exploration for low-history users, and heavier reliance on the session-based path.

IF YOU GET ONE SHOT

The 90-second opening

"I'll assume a video home feed: 70M DAU, ~60k peak QPS, 80M eligible items, 300 ms p99. The business objective is retention, which isn't trainable, so I'll optimize a per-impression surrogate — a weighted combination of calibrated engagement and satisfaction probabilities — and tune those weights by A/B against retention.

Scoring 80M items per request is impossible, so it's a funnel: an ensemble of retrieval sources cuts 80M to about 4,000, a pre-ranker to 600, a multi-task DNN ranks those, and a policy layer applies diversity and hard constraints to produce 30. Baseline is popularity plus co-visitation, then a GBDT ranker; I'd only go to DNNs for content-based cold start, multi-task heads, and sequence features.

The three things I'd expect to actually go wrong: training/serving skew; position and popularity bias feeding back into the training data, which is why I want a random-traffic holdout; and cold start for new items, which I'd handle with a content tower plus a Thompson-sampled exploration budget. Where should I go deep?"
