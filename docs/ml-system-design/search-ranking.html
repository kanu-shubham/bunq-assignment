01

Clarify & scope

Five minutes · one objective, one metric

Do not start designing. Ask five questions, and say out loud what each answer changes — that second half is what makes it look like experience rather than a memorized checklist.

What is search for here?

"Relevance" is not a business objective. Marketplaces want conversion per session. Content platforms want session depth and retention. Support search wants deflection — the user finds the answer instead of filing a ticket. Enterprise search wants time-to-first-useful-result.

Pick one and commit:

Commit to this sentence

Maximize the probability that a search session ends in a purchase, without regressing p99 latency or seller diversity.

Everything downstream — the label, the loss, the guardrails — is derived from that sentence. If the interviewer won't pick, state your assumption and move; blocking here burns the clock.

Translate it into an ML metric

Session conversion isn't optimizable per request, so decompose it: session conversion → the user finds a relevant item high in the list → NDCG@10 on graded engagement labels, with purchase-weighted gains.

Then name the gap out loud: NDCG going up does not guarantee GMV goes up. That gap is precisely what the A/B test exists to measure. Saying it in minute four buys you credit for the rest of the hour.

Scope cuts, stated explicitlyEnglish only for v1. Web and mobile share one ranker with a surface feature. No personalization for logged-out users beyond session context. Ads and organic are ranked separately and blended by a downstream policy layer — mixing them into one model is a different and harder problem.

02

Frame as ML

Four minutes · label, granularity, loss

Three questions, in this order. This is the part beginners skip and senior candidates dwell on.

What is the label?

You do not have relevance labels. You have logs. Construct graded relevance from engagement:

SignalGradeNotes
Purchase / booking4Sparse, but closest to the objective
Add to cart / save3Roughly 10× denser than purchase
Click, dwell ≥ 30 s2Dwell threshold tuned per vertical
Click, bounce < 10 s0–1A bad click — the title worked, the item didn't. Score it as positive and you have trained a clickbait engine
Impression, no click0Only if the item was actually viewed

Two rules separate a real label design from a toy one:

  1. Only viewed impressions are negatives. An item rendered at position 30 that the user never scrolled to is not a negative — it is missing data. Log a viewport signal and drop everything below the deepest viewed position.
  2. Bad clicks must be distinguishable from no clicks. Dwell-gating is the cheapest defense against optimizing for the thumbnail instead of the item.

Add a human-rated golden set: a few thousand (query, item) pairs graded 0–4 by trained raters against a written guideline, refreshed quarterly. Too small to train on, essential to evaluate on — it is the only label source not contaminated by what your current ranker chose to show. An LLM judge can extend it cheaply for pre-launch checks; calibrate it against human raters first, and never let it be the only ground truth.

What is one training example?

Not one (query, item) row. One example is one slate — a query-session plus the full list of viewed candidates with their labels:

example = {
  qid:  (query_text, user_id, timestamp, surface),
  docs: [(features₁, grade₁, position₁),
         (features₂, grade₂, position₂), … ],
}

Granularity matters three times over: listwise losses need the group; the train/test split must be by query group and by time or the same slate leaks across the boundary; and NDCG is computed per group and averaged, so grouping is what makes the loss and the metric agree.

What is the loss?

Ranking is not classification. Three families, and you should be able to say why you'd move between them:

  • Pointwise — predict a grade per item, MSE or cross-entropy. Works with any classifier, but optimizes absolute score rather than order, and lets easy high-candidate queries dominate the gradient. Fine as a baseline; required if you also need a calibrated probability for an auction or a blend.
  • Pairwise — for each pair where gradei > gradej in a group, learn P(i above j) = σ(si − sj) and optimize cross-entropy (RankNet). Models order directly. Weakness: every pair counts equally, so fixing ranks 30 vs 40 gets the same gradient as 1 vs 2 — which the metric does not care about.
  • Listwise — optimize a list-level objective. LambdaRank is the practical answer: take the RankNet gradient and scale it by the NDCG change from swapping that pair.
λij  =  −σ / (1 + e σ(si − sj))  ·  |ΔNDCGij| The |ΔNDCG| factor is the whole trick — it concentrates the gradient on the swaps that move the metric, which are the ones near the top of the list.

Feed those gradients into gradient-boosted trees and you have LambdaMART — still the default production ranker for tabular ranking features, and the right name to say here. Softmax cross-entropy over the slate (ListNet / ListMLE) is the neural equivalent, and what you use once the features go dense.

Say this

"I'd start pointwise because it's the fastest path to a working end-to-end system, then move to LambdaMART — the metric I committed to is NDCG@10, and LambdaMART optimizes a smooth surrogate of exactly that."

Metrics

Five minutes · offline, online, guardrails

Three tiers. Naming all three — and knowing that they routinely disagree — is the whole point of this section.

Offline

MetricStageWhy it's there
Recall@k (k=500–1000)RetrievalThe ceiling on everything downstream. If the item isn't in the candidate set, no ranker can recover it. Measure per retrieval source
NDCG@10RankingPrimary. Position-discounted, handles graded labels. Report @1, @5, @10 — @1 moves differently and users feel it most
MRRRankingFor navigational queries where exactly one answer is right ("track my order"), NDCG is the wrong lens
Precision@1RankingThe single most user-visible number in the system

Always sliceReport every offline metric by head / torso / tail query, new vs returning user, mobile vs web, and intent class. A +2% aggregate NDCG that comes entirely from head queries while tail regresses is a bad launch — slicing is how you find that before the A/B does.

Online

  • Primary: purchases per search session.
  • Engagement: CTR@top-3, mean reciprocal click position, click depth.
  • Failure proxies: query reformulation rate ↓ (a reformulation means you failed), zero-click session rate ↓, time-to-first-click ↓.
  • Long-horizon: searches per user per week, 7- and 28-day retention. Badly underpowered in a two-week test — say so, and read them from a holdback instead.

04

Data

Six minutes · logging, sampling, bias, leakage

Sources

Search logs are the training set — query, user, timestamp, the full ranked slate with positions, viewport events, clicks, downstream conversions. They are also produced by the ranker you're replacing, which is the central problem of the entire field. Add the catalog (text, attributes, price, stock, seller), query logs (reformulation chains, spelling corrections, autocomplete acceptances), and the human-rated golden set.

Log the features, not just the IDs

Log-and-serve is the single most effective defense against train/serve skew: write out the feature vector exactly as it was computed at request time. Recompute it later from warehouse tables and you will silently train on a price that has since changed, a CTR that includes the future, or a user profile that already saw the result. Logging feature vectors costs storage and saves months.

Sampling

  • Negatives come from the same slate, never from random catalog items. A random negative is trivially separable and teaches nothing about the decisions the ranker actually faces. (The exception is the retrieval two-tower model, which does want easy in-batch negatives plus mined hard ones — different stage, different distribution.)
  • Downsample zero-engagement slates. They're ~70% of traffic and mostly carry no ranking signal. Keep a sample; reweight if you need calibrated outputs.
  • Cap per-query contribution so one head query can't dominate — weight by log(frequency) or cap slates per query per day.
  • Window: 3–6 months with recency weighting. Longer captures seasonality; too long encodes a catalog that no longer exists.

Position bias — raise this before you're asked

Users click position 1 because it is position 1. Under the examination hypothesis:

P(click | q, d, k)  =  P(examine | k)  ·  P(relevant | q, d) The first term is the propensity pk — a property of the position, not the item. The second is the only thing you want to learn.

Train naively and you learn "whatever the old ranker put on top is good" — a self-fulfilling loop that freezes the system in place. Three fixes, increasing in cost:

  1. Position as a feature, zeroed at inference. Cheap, partially effective, standard practice. The model attributes the position effect to the position feature, and you hold it constant when serving.
  2. Inverse propensity scoring. Weight each example by 1/pk. Estimate pk by swapping adjacent pairs on 1% of traffic (cheap, unbiased), or by regression-EM over the natural rank variation across sessions. Clip the weights — the variance will bite.
  3. Two-tower debiasing. One tower learns bias from position and context, one learns relevance; keep only the relevance tower at serving time.

Name the siblings too: trust bias (users believe top results, so clicks there are inflated even when the item is wrong) and selection bias (you only ever observe what the old retrieval stage surfaced — which is why a new retrieval stage is nearly impossible to evaluate offline).

Leakage

The one that quietly kills projectsPoint-in-time correctness. item_ctr_7d must be computed as of the request timestamp. Build it from a current snapshot and the feature includes clicks caused by the very impression you're predicting — offline NDCG looks spectacular and the A/B is flat. Split by time, too: train on weeks 1–8, validate on 9, test on 10. A random row split leaks the future into the past through every aggregate feature you have.

Imbalance and delayed feedback

Purchases are ~1% of clicks. Handle it with graded labels — a purchase is grade 4, not a separate binary task — rather than resampling, which distorts calibration. And conversions arrive late: a purchase can land days after the search. Choose an attribution window (24h same-session, 7d for the tail), accept that the freshest data is incomplete, and either wait out the window before training or model the delay explicitly. At minimum, never train on a partially-attributed final day and then wonder why the conversion features look depressed.

Features

Five minutes · four families, one correctness rule

Four families. Interviewers are listening for whether you reach the interaction family, because that is where the signal actually lives.

FamilyExamples
QueryLength, language, detected intent (navigational / informational / transactional), spell-corrected form, extracted entities (brand, category, size, color, price constraint), frequency bucket, historical query-level CTR, embedding
ItemPrice, discount, rating count and mean, age, stock, seller tier, image quality, category, text embedding, historical CTR and conversion — smoothed toward a category prior, or a one-impression-one-click item scores 100%
User & contextDevice, surface, time of day, locale, queries so far this session, recently viewed categories, long-term affinity embedding, logged-in state, price-tier affinity. Cold users back off to segment averages — never to NaN
Interaction
query × item		Lexical: BM25 score, exact title match, query-term coverage, match field (title vs description vs reviews). Semantic: query–item embedding cosine, cross-encoder score if the budget allows. Behavioral: historical CTR for this (query, item) pair and for (query-cluster, item) — usually the strongest single feature in the model, and also the most exposed to feedback loops and point-in-time leakage. Personal: user-affinity–item similarity, distance from the user's usual price tier

The rule that governs all of themEvery aggregate above needs point-in-time correctness. The clean implementation is a feature store with as-of joins for training and a low-latency online store serving the same transformation code. If training and serving compute a feature through two different code paths, they will diverge — and the divergence surfaces as "great offline, flat in the A/B," the most expensive failure mode in this entire design.

06

Model

Eight minutes · baseline, then two stages, then depth

100M items, 200 ms. You cannot score 100M items — nobody can. So the architecture is decided before the model is:

Retrieval, in detail

BM25 is the lexical backbone — free, interpretable, and unbeatable on rare exact tokens like "iPhone 14 Pro 256GB":

score(q,d)  =  Σt∈q   IDF(t)  ·  f(t,d)(k₁+1)  /  [ f(t,d) + k₁(1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl) ] k₁ ≈ 1.2 controls term-frequency saturation, b ≈ 0.75 the length normalization. It fails on vocabulary mismatch: "laptop for kids" never matches "children's notebook computer."

Semantic retrieval exists to fix exactly that failure. A two-tower dual encoder trains a query tower and an item tower to place engaged pairs close together, with in-batch negatives and a sampled-softmax loss. Two details worth naming, because they're where it goes wrong:

  • logQ correction. In-batch negatives oversample popular items, so subtract log(sampling probability) from the logits — otherwise the model learns popularity, not relevance.
  • Hard negative mining. Retrieve top-k with the current model, take the non-engaged results as negatives, retrain. This is what moves Recall@k; random negatives alone plateau early.

Item embeddings are precomputed offline — nightly full rebuild plus streaming updates for new items — and loaded into the ANN index. The query tower runs per request, so it has to be small: a distilled 4–6 layer transformer, or a bag-of-embeddings model on the tightest budgets.

Ship hybrid, not either/or: BM25 first, semantic added as a second source. Reciprocal rank fusion — Σ 1/(60 + rank_i) — is a strong, tuning-free way to combine the two lists.

Cold startNew items have no engagement features. Back off to content features and category priors, and reserve a small exploration budget — ε-greedy slots or Thompson sampling over a score-uncertainty estimate — so new inventory can earn its first impressions. Without it, the ranker structurally cannot discover anything it hasn't already shown.

07

Serving

Six minutes · a budget that adds up

When it doesn't fit, cut candidates before you cut model quality — going 1 000 → 500 costs less NDCG than dropping the ranker. Then add the pre-rank stage, then distill. And give every stage a timeout with a defined fallback: on ranker timeout, return the retrieval-order list. A search box that returns something imperfect beats one that returns an error.

Precompute vs. real-time

Precomputed offlineComputed per request
Item embeddings, ANN indexQuery embedding & query understanding
Item aggregates — CTR, conversion, qualitySession-context features
User long-term affinity embeddingsPrice, stock, eligibility — must be fresh; never serve an out-of-stock item because the index was stale
Head-query result listsRanking scores

Caching

The query distribution is Zipfian, so caching pays extraordinarily well. Cache query understanding (Redis, ~1h TTL) for a near-total hit rate on head queries. Cache full results keyed by (normalized query, filters, user segment) with a 5–15 minute TTL — never key by raw user ID, which collapses the hit rate and risks leaking personalization across users. Cache query embeddings within a session. Invalidate on price and stock change, or accept staleness and re-verify availability at render time.

The ANN index

HNSW hits the quality/latency sweet spot — M=32, efConstruction=200, efSearch tuned until recall against exact search is ≥0.95 (measure it, don't assume it). Memory is the real constraint: 100M × 768 float32 ≈ 300 GB. So project down to 128–256 dimensions, quantize to int8 for ~4× with negligible recall loss, or move to IVF-PQ / ScaNN if memory dominates. Shard by item ID across replicas, rebuild nightly with incremental updates for new items, and blue/green swap so a bad rebuild never takes serving down.

Version the pairPin the model and index versions together in one deployable. They co-evolve — an embedding model rollback with a stale index gives you a system that is quietly scoring in two different vector spaces.

08

Evaluation & rollout

Four minutes · four gates, four questions

Four gates. Each answers a different question, and each can stop the launch on its own.

GATE 1

Offline replay

Score held-out logged slates; NDCG@10 and Recall@1000, sliced. Fast, cheap, and biased — it can only tell you how well you reorder what the old system retrieved. For a new retrieval stage it's nearly blind. Use IPS/SNIPS with clipped weights when the new policy reorders heavily.

Does it rank better on yesterday's traffic?
GATE 2

Shadow traffic

Run the new pipeline on live traffic, serve the old results, log both for a few days. This validates infrastructure, not quality: peak latency, error rates, feature availability — and train/serve skew, by comparing shadow feature distributions against training data.

Does it survive production?
GATE 3

Interleaving

Blend both rankings into one list (team-draft) and attribute clicks to the source ranker. Roughly 10–100× more sensitive than an A/B because it controls for user variance — a directional read in hours, not weeks. It measures preference, not business impact, so it triages candidates rather than deciding them.

Which ranker do users prefer?
GATE 4

A/B test

The only gate that measures the business objective. At 3% baseline conversion, a 2% relative lift at 80% power needs ≈1.5M sessions per arm — two weeks at 1% traffic, two days at 10%. Randomize by user, never by request. Read guardrails before the primary metric.

Does it make money without breaking anything?

Ramp: 1% → 5% → 25% → 50% → 100%, holding at each step long enough to read guardrails, with a 1–5% holdback kept for a month to measure retention and catch slow feedback-loop damage. Automate rollback on guardrail breach, and keep a kill switch that reverts model and index together in one action.

Novelty effectEngagement often spikes for a few days on any visible change. Discount the first 3–7 days. If the lift is gone by week two, it was novelty, not quality — and a launch decision made on day three is a coin flip.

Two minutes · drift, loops, triggers

Position bias is not solved once. Re-estimate propensities periodically — the examination curve is a property of the UI, so a layout redesign silently invalidates every propensity you trained on.

The feedback loopThe ranker chooses what is shown; what is shown becomes tomorrow's training data. Left alone, this concentrates traffic on already-popular items, starves new inventory, and slowly narrows the catalog — while every offline metric looks fine. Counter it with a permanent exploration budget (a randomized slot, or Thompson sampling on score uncertainty), the coverage guardrail from §03, and periodic randomized-traffic collection that yields genuinely unbiased evaluation data. Roughly 1% of traffic, and you defend it in every planning meeting, because it is the price of being able to learn anything new.

Retraining triggers — cadence and condition

  • Scheduled: GBDT weekly; two-tower monthly, with a nightly index refresh.
  • Triggered: golden-set NDCG drops >2%, PSI breaches on a top-10 feature, or a catalog/UI change big enough to shift the feature distribution.
  • Always gated: every retrain goes through offline → shadow → A/B. A retrain is a new model, not a config change — auto-retrain wired to auto-deploy with no quality gate is how one bad data day reaches production.

Signals & traps

What earns the level, and what loses it

Reads as senior

  • Naming the objective→metric gap before designing anything, and treating the A/B as the arbiter of it.
  • Choosing the simplest model that meets the constraint and defending it — LambdaMART over a transformer, with the latency budget as the argument.
  • Raising position bias and feedback loops unprompted.
  • Point-in-time correctness and log-and-serve, with the failure mode named: "great offline, flat online."
  • A latency budget with slack in it and a defined fallback per stage.
  • Guardrails that can block a launch — including one that protects the ecosystem rather than the user.
  • Sequencing: what ships this quarter versus what is a dedicated project.
