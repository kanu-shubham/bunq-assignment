Distributed systems 14

What does a timeout tell you?

Nothing about whether the work happened. It's an ambiguity, not a failure.

Can you get exactly-once delivery?

No. At-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer gives exactly-once effect.

Why can't you write to the DB and publish to Kafka atomically?

Dual-write problem — two systems, two failure modes. No ordering is safe. Fix: transactional outbox.

Why not use XA / two-phase commit?

Every participant must support it, locks are held across a network round trip, and a coordinator crash leaves rows locked in doubt.

What does an idempotency key identify?

The client's intent — so every retry of one logical payment carries the same key.

Why can't check-then-insert work?

It's a race. Two requests both read "absent", both insert. Only a database constraint can arbitrate — application locking can't, across instances.

Why hash the request body?

To catch a client reusing a key for a different payment. Otherwise you return the first payment's response and they believe the second succeeded.

What if you crash between claim and complete?

The key is stranded IN_PROGRESS. A reaper releases it — with a threshold longer than the work's worst case, including its retries.

Do you need global event ordering?

Almost never. Per-entity ordering via partitioning on the entity id. Global ordering means a single writer and no scaling.

How do consumers survive out-of-order events?

Version numbers, and a state machine that rejects impossible transitions. The state machine is more robust — it's a domain rule, not plumbing.

State CAP precisely.

On partition, choose availability or consistency. It says nothing about normal operation. PACELC is the better framing: else, choose latency or consistency.

Where does a ledger sit on that?

CP. Refusing a payment is annoying; double-spending isn't recoverable.

Compensation vs rollback?

You can't un-send a payment. You send a return — a new transaction, visible on the customer's statement.

Orchestration or choreography for payments?

Orchestration. When a regulator asks what happened to a payment, you need one place that knows.

Fault tolerance 12

Why is a slow dependency worse than a dead one?

Dead fails fast. Slow holds threads until the pool exhausts — then every endpoint dies, including ones unrelated to it.

How do you size a timeout?

Downstream p99 plus headroom. And timeouts must shrink going down the stack, never grow.

What must you never retry?

Deterministic failures — 400, 401, 403, 422. The answer won't change, and under load it's waste aimed at a system already saying no.

Why jitter?

Backoff alone synchronises the herd: everyone fails at once, waits the same interval, and retries at once. Randomness disperses them.

Why keep retry budgets small?

They multiply. Three services deep at three attempts each is 27 requests at the bottom.

Why does a circuit breaker need HALF_OPEN?

To probe recovery without handing a fragile service the entire backlog the instant it comes back.

Why a minimum call count before tripping?

Otherwise the breaker trips on tiny samples during quiet periods — three failures out of four at 04:00 is noise, not an outage.

Should slow calls count as failures?

Yes. An eight-second response is functionally down, and error-only counting misses the most common real outage shape.

Should business rejections trip the breaker?

No. The partner is healthy and answering correctly. One partner's bad data would cut off every other partner's payments.

What does a bulkhead protect?

Everything else. It stops one dependency draining a shared pool.

What bounds total request time?

Not per-attempt timeouts — deadline propagation from the caller. Most resilience configs get this wrong.

Liveness vs readiness?

"Restart me" vs "can I serve traffic now". Conflating them turns a brief database blip into a simultaneous restart of every pod.

The composition question

Anyone can list the patterns. The one that separates candidates is what order they nest in.

1 · Bulkhead

Outermost, because its concurrency cap must cover the retries and the backoff waits between them. A retrying call still occupies a slot.

2 · Retry

Outside the breaker, so the breaker records every individual attempt — it sees the true failure rate, trips fast, and then short-circuits the remaining retries.

3 · Circuit breaker

Counts outcomes, sheds load when open. Business rejections excluded from its window.

4 · Time limiter

Innermost, so each attempt gets its own deadline. Outside the retry, one slow call eats the whole budget and the retries never run.

the actual call

Carrying an idempotency key that is stable across every attempt — which is the only reason retrying is safe at all.

Retry these

  • 503, 502, connection reset
  • Read timeout (ambiguous — safe only because of the idempotency key)
  • 429, honouring Retry-After

Never retry these

  • 400 malformed request
  • 401 / 403 auth
  • 422 business rejection — "beneficiary account closed"

Java, Spring & JPA 9

Nobody will test your Stream API recall at lead level. These are the ones that come up because they bite in production.

The biggest @Transactional trap?

Self-invocation. Spring uses a proxy; a call from one method of a class to another goes straight to this and the annotation is silently ignored. Same for @Async and @Cacheable.

Which exceptions roll back by default?

Unchecked only. A checked exception commits unless you set rollbackFor.

What is REQUIRES_NEW for?

An independent transaction that commits immediately — how an idempotency claim becomes visible to competing requests before the slow work starts.

What is open-in-view, and why disable it?

It defaults to true and holds a DB connection for the entire HTTP request including response serialisation. Under load it exhausts the pool.

The JPA save() gotcha with assigned IDs?

Non-null id → treated as detached → merge() → a duplicate-key INSERT silently becomes an UPDATE. Fix: implement Persistable so it calls persist().

What does @Version give you?

Optimistic locking. Hibernate appends WHERE version = ?; the second concurrent writer matches zero rows and gets an exception. No locks, no deadlocks, loser retries.

What are virtual threads?

JVM-scheduled threads (Java 21). Millions can exist and blocking is cheap again — which removes most of the reason to reach for reactive.

Their one caveat?

synchronized can pin a virtual thread to its carrier. Prefer ReentrantLock in hot paths.

Simplest concurrency strategy in Java?

Immutability. An object that can't change is automatically thread-safe — which is why your functional habits transfer and are worth more here.

Payments domain 10

The differentiator. Most candidates for a payments role are competent backend engineers who have never thought hard about money.

Why never double for money?

0.1 has no exact binary representation, so 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3 — in Java, JavaScript, everywhere. Fractions of a penny compound into unexplained drift.

How do you store £12.34?

1234 minor units, plus the currency. Money is counted, not measured.

Which currencies break "multiply by 100"?

JPY has 0 decimal places; KWD, BHD and JOD have 3.

Why not send amounts as JSON numbers?

Most parsers make them doubles, so 10.10 can arrive as 10.099999999999999. Send a digit string and parse it yourself.

What does double-entry guarantee?

Every transaction has entries summing to zero, so money is conserved — and that invariant is continuously checkable.

How do you fix a ledger mistake?

A reversing entry. You never update or delete — the error and its correction both stay visible. The audit trail is the product.

Is SETTLED terminal?

No. A beneficiary bank can return funds days later. A model where settled ends the story can't represent that.

Which state is most dangerous?

SUBMITTED. The instruction is on the wire and the outcome is genuinely unknown — it must be reconciled against the partner's record, never inferred from our request.

The worst reconciliation break?

In theirs, not ours — a payment you recorded as failed that actually moved money. Only comparing against the counterparty finds it.

Why is compliance an architecture concern?

Sanctions screening sits in the synchronous path, so its p99 is inside your API's p99. It's a functional requirement with a latency budget, not a gate at the end.

The answer that carries the round

Asked why the accept endpoint returns 202 and does no network I/O: “Calling the partner bank inside the request holds a database transaction across a network call — a partner taking 30 seconds exhausts the connection pool long before it recovers, and takes down every unrelated endpoint. Worse, if the partner call succeeded and our commit then failed, we'd have moved money with no local record of it. So we persist, return 202, and settle asynchronously.” That one answer demonstrates dual writes, pool exhaustion and honest API design at once.
